## Systematic habits: tell the other writers

1. **Invented "why" in explanations.** This is the biggest habit: about half the findings. Writers add a causal or motivating sentence and credit it to the source: "which is why", "mainly because", "This is why OpenTelemetry…", "the primer's own example of why…", "Anything else risks…". Two of these are wrong (span-kind-client, span-kind-consumer). Rule: an explanation may only restate, apply or connect what the cited section says. If it gives a reason, that reason must be in the source.
2. **Dropped hedges.** The sources say "not necessarily", "almost certainly", "usually" and "often". The cards say "not", "means", "exactly the case" and "always". Keep the source's own qualifier.
3. **Closed-list fronts on open lists.** "What two advantages…", "what two other ways…", and a cloze on the last item of a partial list, when the source lists three, thirty or eight. Write "Name two…", or cloze an item that is unique. Say "among others" when the list is partial.
4. **Tier C copied, not paraphrased.** All five SRE-book cards (tier C, facts only) copy the book's sentences or its own example (the 100 ms search latency). CONTENT-POLICY.md §3, tier C applies. Writers treat "cite and paraphrase lightly" as enough. For tier C sources, write the fact from scratch and use an original scenario.
5. **Section-scoped citations that the claim does not fit.** A card cites one anchor while the claim comes from another section or page (metric definition cited to the tracing section). Check the anchor holds the claim.
6. **Examples that give away later cards.** An `example` on a primer card reuses the exact scenario of a later classification card (courier refund). Keep the two apart. autoscaling-needs-sd and autoscaler-needs-kubernetes-sd are also near-twins, so drop or differentiate one.
